Smaller than your thumb. Yet of thousands of keys, only one turns it.
Simplest lock: a plug turns in a shell and pushes the bolt. But a screwdriver turns it too.
So you drill through both and drop in a pin. Stuck. Egyptians did this in wood, four thousand years ago.
But any stick shoves that pin up and out. So you cut it in two. Too low, the top half blocks. Too high, the bottom half. The gap must sit right on the seam: the shear line.
Hold the lock upside down, and gravity can't drop the pins back. So a spring presses each stack down.
One pin is easy to guess. So you use five, each a different height. Your key's cuts are a password of heights.
Flat pins would snag those cuts. So their tips are rounded. The key rides over like speed bumps.
But no factory is perfect. Holes sit slightly off, so pins jam one at a time, and a picker sets each one. So some top pins are spools. The plug gives a little, then stops. A fake win.
A key one cut off: stuck. A pick: stuck. Your key: five gaps, one line. Click.
Now you know how to build a pin tumbler lock.
