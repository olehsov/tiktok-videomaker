No pumps. No engines. Yet water flows thirty-one miles into a city, all by itself.
Easy way: a straight ditch to town. But the land rises, and the water stops.
Water only flows downhill. Too steep, it rips the channel apart. Too flat, it stalls. So you give it a tiny, steady slope: under two feet per mile.
A hill in the way? Water can't go over it. So you wind around it, or tunnel straight through.
An open ditch is a trap: sun, evaporation, dirt, leaves. So you bury it under stone slabs. Most of the way, it runs underground.
Then the soil drinks your water. So you coat the channel with crushed tiles and lime: waterproof, like a bathtub.
Now a valley. Water that drops in won't climb out. So you build arches that send the weight down into the piers. At Pont du Gard: three tiers, a hundred and sixty feet.
Some valleys are too deep for arches. So you seal the water in lead pipes. It rushes down and up the other side, into a tank slightly lower. Water seeks its own level.
After rain, the water turns muddy. Sand would clog every pipe. So you add a settling tank: water slows, sand sinks.
Then the hidden threat: dissolved limestone. Year after year, it coats the walls like kettle scale. So you leave hatches, and crews scrape it clean.
Finally, the city. A round tank splits the flow: fountains, baths, homes.
Autumn storm. The river roars. The mud stays in the tank. The arches hold. The fountains keep running: up to ten million gallons a day, for centuries.
Now you know how to build a Roman aqueduct.
