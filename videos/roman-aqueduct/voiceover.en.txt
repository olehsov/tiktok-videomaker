No pumps. No engines. Yet water flows thirty-one miles into a city, all by itself.
Easy way: a straight ditch to town. But the land rises, and the water stops.
Water only flows downhill. Too steep, it rips the channel apart. Too flat, it stalls. The fix? A tiny, steady slope: under two feet per mile.
A hill in the way? Water can't go over it. So you wind around it, or tunnel straight through.
An open ditch is a trap: sun, evaporation, dirt, leaves. So you bury it under stone slabs. Mostly, it runs underground.
Then the soil drinks your water. So you coat the channel with crushed tiles and lime: waterproof, like a bathtub.
Now a valley. Water that drops in won't climb out. So arches carry the weight down into the piers. Pont du Gard: three tiers, a hundred and sixty feet.
Some valleys are too deep for arches. So you seal it in lead pipes. It rushes down, then up into a tank slightly lower. Water seeks its own level.
After rain, the water turns muddy. Sand would clog every pipe. So you add a settling tank: water slows, sand sinks.
The hidden threat: dissolved limestone. Year after year, it coats the walls like kettle scale. So you leave hatches, and crews scrape it clean.
Finally, the city. A round tank splits the flow: fountains, baths, homes.
Autumn storm. The river roars. Mud stays in the tank. Arches hold. Fountains keep running: up to ten million gallons a day, for centuries.
Now you know how to build a Roman aqueduct.
