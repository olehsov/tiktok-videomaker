A fire. Right under your floor. And you walk on it, warm and safe.
Easy way: a fire in the middle of the room. Smoke in your eyes, soot on the walls, cold feet.
So the fire moves outside. A furnace behind the wall sends heat one way: under the floor.
But the floor sits on solid ground. So you raise it on tile stacks, two feet tall. That’s a hypocaust — heated from below.
But thin tiles scorch over the flames and stay cold elsewhere. So you lay a thick sandwich: tiles, concrete, more tiles. It spreads the heat, like a pizza stone.
The smoke is trapped. It chokes the fire. So you line the walls with hollow box tiles. Hot air rises through them to the roof, pulling the fire, warming the walls.
One fire, three rooms. The air cools as it travels. So the hot room sits by the furnace, the warm room next. The cold room gets none.
But at night the fire dies, and the floor goes cold. So someone feeds it wood all day. Warm floors eat a mountain of firewood.
And one danger you can’t see or smell: carbon monoxide. Through one crack, it seeps into the room. So every joint is sealed, and the draft pulls it out.
January on Hadrian’s Wall. Frost outside. In the hot room, a hundred and four. Floors so hot you wear wooden sandals.
Now you know how to build a hypocaust.
