A bridge of loose stones. No glue, no mortar. Cars still cross it, nineteen hundred years later.
Easy way: one long stone slab across the river. It sags, its belly stretches — and stone hates stretching. Crack.
So you'll use many small stones. First, the pier. On river mud it sinks. So ring the spot with oak stakes and clay, pump it dry, dig to rock.
But loose stones can't float. So carpenters build a wooden half-circle — the centering.
On it you lay stones cut as wedges, wider on top. Square blocks would slip out. A wedge can't. Like a cork.
One gap stays open at the very top. The last wedge drops in — the keystone. Now every stone is locked.
Now the scary part: knock the frame away. Too early, and it all falls. After the keystone, it stands alone.
But an arch never sleeps. It pushes sideways, day and night. Weak sides spread, and the top drops. So each end leans on a massive stone shoulder in the bank.
Then you pile stone on top, up to a flat road. Strange, but that weight squeezes the wedges tighter.
Last enemy: floods. High water slams the bridge. So you leave a hole through the pier. Floodwater runs straight through.
Spring flood. Brown water roars below. Carts rumble overhead. Not one stone moves.
Now you know how to build a Roman arch.
