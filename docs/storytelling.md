# Storytelling rules

## Core rule
Every design element is one chain: **problem → what happens without a fix → solution → why it works.**
Nothing appears "just because". Example: a wide wall base spreads the dome's weight over the soil;
without it the ground settles unevenly, walls crack, the structure tilts and collapses within a few seasons.

The viewer must leave thinking: *"that's just a chain of simple, smart decisions — I could do that."*

## Narrative engine: the "but… / so you…" loop
- Second person: the viewer is the builder ("you dig", "you raise a wall").
- Cold open with the simplest attempt, which breaks immediately ("fill a bucket and wait for night — by morning it's just warm water").
- Then repeat: **"But…"** (new problem) → **"So you…"** (fix). A new conflict every ~8–12 s.
- Stakes rise: small annoyances first, a dangerous or invisible threat near the end.
- Climax = stress test: the finished build survives the worst case (blizzard, July heat, flood).
- Ending: a calm image, the observer-animal callback, and the fixed final line
  **"Now you know how to build a/an <thing>."** No call to action.

## Techniques (use most of them in every video)
1. **Paradox hook** in the first 3 s ("Ice. In the desert. In July.").
2. **Concrete numbers** instead of adjectives (6 logs, half a meter, 104°F, 10 minutes).
3. **Physics in one sentence** ("warm air rises, cold air sinks").
4. **Everyday analogy** per big idea ("like roof tiles", "like a fur coat", "like rebar").
5. **Invisible threat** twist (carbon monoxide, meltwater under the ice).
6. **Failure ghost**: show the failure for 1–1.5 s before the fix.
7. **Stakes meter** in a corner (warmth, ice supply, dryness) that drops on threats, rises on fixes.
8. **Day/night rhythm** to split acts without words.
9. **Scale through a person**: when something is big, show the tiny puppet next to it.
10. **Recipe from ordinary things**: materials drop in one by one ("clay, ash, egg whites, goat hair").
11. **Quick recap** (2–3 s, no voice): all step plates blink over the finished build before the final line.
12. **One historical anchor** as an overlay line (era, where it still stands). Does not break second person.
13. **Recurring observer animal** with a callback at the end.

## Narration style
- Short sentences, 3–10 words. One idea per sentence.
- Plain words. A term is allowed only if explained in the same or next sentence ("That's a qanat — an underground channel").
- Numbers spoken the way people say them ("a hundred and four", "half a meter").
- English pace ≈ 2.5 words/s (ElevenLabs, normal speed) → target words ≈ duration × 2.5 (60 s ≈ 150 words, 90 s ≈ 225, 120 s ≈ 300). Leave room for silent beats and the 1.5 s tail.
- Units: imperial in English narration (°F, feet), metric in `uk`/`ru`; overlays show both where it matters ("104°F / 40°C").

## Topic categories (for variety)
`shelter` · `weapon-tool` · `transport` · `water` · `food-preservation` · `fire-heat` · `cold-tech` · `farming` · `craft-material` · `navigation-signal` · `defense-fortification` · `everyday-object`
Max 2 topics from one category in a batch of 10.

## Good topic test (all must be true)
- Has 4–10 design details that each solve a clear problem.
- The solution chain fits in 60–120 s.
- Can be shown in a side-view cross-section.
- Has a surprising hook (paradox, ancient-but-clever, "why is it shaped like that?").
- Facts are verifiable from at least two sources.
